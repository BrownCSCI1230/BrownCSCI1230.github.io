Introduction

Welcome to Lab 7! As you might know, video, audio, and even electrocardiograms of your heart are all innately continuous signals. However, computers can only store and process a finite collection of values, which means that those continuous signals must be somehow converted into discrete signals (a quick reminder of their differences here).

In this lab, you will explore how we transform signals between continuous and discrete representations, and what information will be preserved, distorted, or otherwise lost when we use different signal processing techniques.

To help simplify your conceptual understanding of this topic, all of the examples used throughout this lab are one-dimensional. Nonetheless, the same ideas apply to multi-dimensional graphics concepts such as image filtering and resizing, which you will be doing in Project 4: Antialias.

Objectives

  1. Understand the Nyquist limit and understand how to choose an appropriate sampling rate to avoid aliasing,
  2. Compare reconstruction filters and describe how they affect the quality of a reconstructed signal,
  3. Interpret a signal in both its spatial (or time) domain and frequency domain, and
  4. Explain the relationship between a signal and its Fourier-domain dual.

Conceptual Background

Signals and Domains

A signal, in simple terms, is a function whose value changes over some input domain. A one-dimensional audio wave, for example, assigns an amplitude to each moment in time (a.k.a. the time domain). Similarly, an image can be viewed as a two-dimensional signal that assigns a color to each location on the image plane (a.k.a. the spatial domain). Note that the terms "time domain" and "spatial domain" both describe the same kind of representation: signal values as a function of their independent variables (time or position, depending on the context of the signal). Since we are mainly working with image processing, we will refer to the input domain as the spatial domain from here on.

Another important concept is the frequency domain, which tells us how much of each frequency is present in the signal. In an image, for example, low frequencies correspond to broad regions of slowly changing color, while high frequencies generally represent sharp edges and fine details.

The Fourier transform converts a signal from its spatial domain to its corresponding frequency domain, both of which represent the same signal but are useful for different purposes.

Sampling and Reconstruction

Sampling converts a continuous signal into a discrete one by recording its value at a finite number of locations. The sampling rate determines how many samples we take per unit of the input domain. You will learn about the Nyquist limit and its relation to the sampling frequency below.

To display and use sampled data, we also need to construct values between samples to make up for the lost parts when transforming a continuous signal into a discrete one. This process is called reconstruction.

Filtering and Fourier Duality

Lastly, a filter changes a signal by preserving some frequencies and suppressing others. A low-pass filter keeps low frequencies and removes high frequencies, producing a smoother or blurrier signal. On the other hand, a high-pass filter emphasizes rapid changes. Before sampling, we often apply a low-pass filter to remove details with high frequencies that the sampling process cannot represent after reconstruction. This is called pre-filtering.

Connecting back to the concept of multiple domains previously, operations in one domain have related effects in the other. Specifically, multiplication in the frequency domain corresponds to convolution in the spatial domain. Thus, multiplying a frequency spectrum by a low-pass filter is equivalent to convolving the original signal with a spatial-domain blur kernel. This relationship is known as the Fourier duals: a function in one domain has a corresponding function in the other. Please refer to the lecture slides if you need a refresher on this topic.
